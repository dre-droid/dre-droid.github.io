MS thesis · AIDA / Niulinx · in progress

Self-supervised pretraining for urban autonomy

Labelling 3D boxes across LiDAR sweeps and their matching camera frames is slow and expensive. The raw driving data is free and piling up. The signal is already there — it just has no labels on it.

My thesis pretrains on unlabelled LiDAR and camera jointly, adapting UniTR — one transformer over both modalities rather than two backbones fused late.

This comes with some challenges as well as many ideas to solve them: how do you create labels yourself? What should the model learn and how to make sure that it actually does? A simple idea is masked reconstruction, make the model predict a corrupted signal - but this comes with the cost of learning noise; a more sophisticated approach is self-distillation - have two copies of the model and feed them two views of the same scene, forcing their embeddings to match; clever but even more susceptible to issues like collapsing representations, shortcuts and stability over long training horizons. These issues aren't unique to driving, they're general problems in SSL (self supervised learning). In AVs though the hard part is structural, there are multiple input modalities. LiDAR is sparse, irregular, metric; camera is dense, regular, projective. Fusing them means choosing where in the network they meet, and that choice constrains everything after it.

What surprised me though is how much of the difficulty is experimental rather than conceptual. Evaluation gets expensive enough that ordinary practice breaks down: instead of early-stopping on a patience counter, you may have to commit to an epoch budget up front and accept leaving performance on the table. A simple evaluation may not be so simple, nor cheap.

Perception is an ubiquitous problem: depth and RGB fused without labels isn't specific to driving. It's close to what a headset solves indoors.

Google Summer of Code 2024 · FFmpeg

Improving perceptual similarity metrics

Selected for Google Summer of Code to implement enhanced SSIM in FFmpeg — the video infrastructure sitting underneath effectively every device that plays video.

The direct implementation evaluates every overlapping window independently: O(mnk²) for an m×n frame and a k×k window, so it degrades as the window grows. I rebuilt it around summed-area tables, which reduce any window to four lookups no matter how large it is — taking the window dimension out of the cost entirely.

I also added Minkowski pooling at p=3 and p=4, configurable window size and stride, and multithreading, then took the patch through review on ffmpeg-devel.

Leonardo Drone Contest 2025 · 2nd nationwide

A ground vehicle, a drone, and a camera that had to agree

A UGV, a UAV and a fixed PTZ camera cooperating to navigate an unknown arena and identify targets, with no human in the loop.

I owned the PTZ task — making a wall-mounted Pan-Tilt-Zoom camera an active sensor that had to locate and decode 30 × 30 cm QR codes placed at random in the field. That meant a policy to scan the arena intelligently and zoom in only on rough detections — enough to find the codes in time, and to see them well enough to decode them.

University lab project · RL · 3D printing · Raspberry Pi

Furuta pendulum, from print to policy

A rotary inverted pendulum built end to end: 3D-printed mechanism, wiring and controller, with a swing-up and balance policy trained in Stable-Baselines3 and deployed on a Raspberry Pi.

Policies trained in simulation didn't survive contact with the hardware; training directly on the rig did. That gap — between a controller that works in simulation and one that works on a bench — is the part of robotics I find most worth understanding.

It also taught me something simulation can't. The safety sensor was mounted on the thing it was protecting: during one unattended run the rig spun out, tore free of the sensor watching for exactly that condition, and kept going at full speed.

Fault detection has to survive the fault. Anything sharing a failure mode with what it monitors isn't monitoring it.
